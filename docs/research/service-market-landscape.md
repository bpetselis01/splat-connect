# SPLAT Connect: Service Market Landscape

**Research date:** 2026-10-02  
**Geographic lens:** Australia first, with international comparators where they serve similar needs.  
**Purpose:** Identify current substitutes and competitors for SPLAT's open guides, second-hand toy exchange, community 3D-printing service, and filament-credit recycling.

## Executive Summary

The market is fragmented. Australian families can find toy libraries, toy swaps, volunteer assistive-technology services, paid print marketplaces, and a few emerging filament-recycling programs, but these generally solve separate parts of the journey. The closest end-to-end analogue is Canada's Makers Making Change, which connects an assistive-device library to volunteer makers and requests. No provider reviewed combines an accessible-play guide library, parent-to-parent adapted-toy exchange, a verified local print-provider network, and earned recycling credit spendable on those prints.

That combination is SPLAT's potential edge, not an established moat. Several competitors have larger networks, long-running charity programs, or existing community access. SPLAT will need reliable local supply, accessible and safe instructions, transparent costs, and trust between families and makers to make the integration valuable.

## SPLAT's Current Offer

The repo contains working mobile/web flows for:

- A public guide library covering toy adaptations and assistive technology, with searchable content and build files.
- A toy library where people and organisations can offer toys as gifts or swaps, with condition, availability, switch-adaptation and location information.
- Requests to verified community printers for parts attached to a guide. The family sees the printer's filament-rate estimate and pays the printer directly; SPLAT does not collect that money.
- Recycling drop-off bookings with participating organisations. A recycler records accepted weight and issues print credit; the contributor cannot self-claim or transfer it. The platform's default minimum is 2 kg, while individual organisations can set their own terms.

The app also contains child profiles and guide-fit matching, saved items, tutorial contribution/collaboration, organisations, maker-wanted requests, challenges, learning content, notifications, and print-job threads. These are additional product capabilities to include in future positioning.

Code evidence: [guide library](../../packages/mobile/components/home/library-screen.tsx), [toy library](../../packages/mobile/components/toys/toy-library-screen.tsx), [print matching](../../packages/mobile/components/printing/find-screen.tsx), [print request and direct-cost terms](../../packages/mobile/components/printing/request-screen.tsx), [recycling credits](../../packages/mobile/components/recycling/recycling-screen.tsx). The presence of these flows in the code does not establish current provider density, local availability, or transaction volume.

## Competitive Position by Feature

### 1. Open guides and free education

**Most direct overlap:** Makers Making Change, Free 3D Hands, TADSA projects, and general model repositories.  
**Potential edge:** SPLAT can make the instructions specific to adapted play and connect the guide to the exact toy, child's access context, reusable toy listing, and print service.  
**Risk/limit:** content is only useful if plans are technically clear, accessible, licensed correctly, safely bounded, and maintained. Other charities may offer free devices and deeper expert review.

### 2. Toy exchange and rotation

**Most direct overlap:** Toy Swap Australia/Joy of Giving; Toy Libraries Australia as a borrowing substitute; FairPlay for adapted-toy access.  
**Potential edge:** family-to-family reuse without needing to wait for an event or join a local library, with adapted/access facts and build instructions attached to the item.  
**Risk/limit:** SPLAT's peer exchange has to solve trust, toy safety, cleaning, handoff and uneven local inventory. Toy libraries already solve inventory, lending periods, maintenance and accessible community support in many locations.

### 3. Community printing service

**Most direct overlap:** Printit4Me and 3D Printing Near Me for Australian provider matching; MMC, Access3D and TADSA for assistive-technology context.  
**Potential edge:** guide-linked STL selection, capacity/material/bed-fit checks, verified provider status, family customization notes, transparent filament estimates and post-print fit feedback in one flow.  
**Risk/limit:** the network only works when there are nearby providers with suitable materials, quality and response times. Verification does not itself certify a design as clinically appropriate or safe.

### 4. Recycling for print credit

**Most direct overlap:** Infilament and Recycl3D; Standard Print Co and TerraCycle are collection/disposal alternatives.  
**Potential edge:** earn local credit for sorted material, then spend it with the participating service provider on an assistive guide part. That is a more purpose-specific incentive than general filament-store credit.  
**Risk/limit:** SPLAT relies on each recycler's equipment, accepted polymers, minimum amounts and available credit budget. Its current 2 kg minimum excludes small household quantities unless a drop-off partner aggregates material.

## Other Features Worth Including in SPLAT's Market Story

The app's implemented routes show additional differentiators beyond the four recalled features: personalized child-fit matching, tutorial contributions/collaborators, printer verification, print-job messaging and fit feedback, organisations, maker-wanted requests, challenges, learning resources, notifications, and saved guides/toys. These may matter to positioning, but only promote a service as available where the route, operational partner and support process are live.

## Bottom Line

SPLAT's best position is **an Australian accessible-play circularity platform**: learn how an adaptation works, find or exchange a suitable toy, request a local print, and return eligible plastic for credit toward future prints. That is more differentiated than claiming to be a better toy library, print shop, recycling company, or open-file repository in isolation.

The evidence does not establish that SPLAT is cheaper overall. Its print requests still pass material cost to families; toy library memberships can be inexpensive; some charities absorb all recipient costs through donations; and recycling credit depends on partner economics. The strongest claim today is integration and accessibility context, conditional on service supply and quality controls.
